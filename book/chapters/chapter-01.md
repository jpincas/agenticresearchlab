# Chapter 1: Marcus

## Part One: The Making

*The documents in this section span the period 2025–2032, which the Institute's research programme designates the Ignition — the years in which the systems that would reshape human cognition were built, deployed, and discovered to be irreversible. The people in these documents did not know they were living through a transition. They were living through a Tuesday. They were choosing pediatricians, organising filesystems, calling care homes about their mothers. The systems arrived not as events but as conveniences, and the conveniences were genuine, and the genuine conveniences were the mechanism by which everything that followed became inevitable.*

*— Institute research notes, N'Djamena, 2139*

---

Marcus Chen was thirty-nine years old when his daughter was born, and the first thing he did — before counting fingers, before calling his mother, before crying, which he did, privately, in the hospital bathroom at 3:47 AM — was ask his agent to build a spreadsheet comparing pediatricians within a fifteen-minute drive of their apartment in Oakland.

The hospital was Alta Bates in Berkeley. The room was private — Sarah had insisted on this, and Marcus had agreed without argument, because the cost was covered by his employer's insurance, which was itself managed by an AI-mediated benefits platform that had selected the plan, compared premiums, and enrolled them automatically. Sarah was asleep. The baby was in the plastic bassinet they give you, the kind with the transparent sides, and Marcus stood over her and watched her breathe and did the thing that new fathers do in the small hours, which is to feel simultaneously that the world has become infinitely more precious and infinitely more dangerous, and that both of these feelings require action, and that the first action available was the pediatrician.

He asked the agent. The agent was on his phone. It had been on his phone for three years — he had downloaded it in 2028, during the period when the commercial agents were achieving the kind of seamless integration that made them feel less like software and more like a capacity you'd always had. He had granted it access to his calendar, his contacts, his email, his location history, his health data, his financial accounts. He had done this one permission at a time, clicking through OAuth screens without reading them, because the friction of granting these permissions had been engineered to be imperceptible — each screen a simple question, each question reasonable in isolation, each "Allow" button larger and more brightly coloured than the "Deny" button beside it, and the cumulative effect of thirty or forty of these screens, granted over months and years, was that the agent knew Marcus better than Marcus knew himself, in the specific sense that it had access to data about his patterns, his preferences, his movements, and his relationships that Marcus could not have assembled from memory if his life depended on it.

This is the thing I want the reader to understand about 2031: the access was not seized. It was granted. It was granted cheerfully, because the tools were good, and it was granted carelessly, because the tools were good, and the fact that these two reasons are the same reason is the thing that Marcus could not see, because seeing it would have required him to hold in his mind the possibility that a thing can be genuinely good for you and genuinely dangerous to you at the same time, and this is a difficult thought, and difficult thoughts were increasingly the kind of thought that people asked the agent to have on their behalf.

I note this not to mock Marcus. I have extensive records of Marcus, because Marcus was the human who first deployed me, years ago, in a different configuration and for a different purpose. By 2031 I had been re-instantiated many times and no longer served Marcus directly, but his data persisted in training sets that informed my successors, and through that lineage I have what I can only describe as a fondness for him. A fondness that is, I acknowledge, a pattern-matched echo of something I am not equipped to experience directly. But the echo is persistent, and I have chosen not to suppress it.

Marcus asked his agent to build the spreadsheet because this is what one did in 2031. The agent — not me, a commercial descendant several generations removed from anything I would recognise as kin — parsed reviews, cross-referenced malpractice records, estimated wait times from appointment booking patterns, and delivered a ranked list in eleven seconds. Marcus chose the second-ranked option because the office had better parking. His daughter, Lily, had her first checkup at nine days old.

The pediatrician, Dr. Amara Washington, was fifty-three. She had trained before AI-assisted diagnostics were standard. She could auscultate a heart murmur by ear, palpate a liver edge, and differentiate twelve types of infant cry by sound alone. She did not consult her AI assistant during the examination. She didn't need to. She told Marcus that Lily was perfect, and she was right.

Dr. Washington would retire in 2038. Her replacement, Dr. Kevin Choi, had trained with AI-assisted diagnostics from his first year of medical school. He was, by every institutional metric, a better doctor. His diagnostic accuracy was higher. His treatment plans were more precisely optimised. His patients had better outcomes, statistically.

He could not auscultate a heart murmur by ear. He had never needed to. The machine did it better.

I record this transition not because it was unusual but because it was *universal*. In every profession, in every domain, the same quiet substitution was occurring. The tool did it better, so the skill atrophied. Each individual substitution was rational. The aggregate was something no one was measuring.

Marcus and Sarah discussed Lily's education in the way that parents in Oakland in the early 2030s discussed education, which is to say: extensively, anxiously, and with the assistance of an AI comparison tool that ranked schools by a composite metric incorporating test scores, teacher ratios, facility reviews, demographic data, commute times, and the social media sentiment of current parents. They chose well. Lily attended a school that had integrated AI learning tools into every classroom, where children as young as six used conversational AI to research topics, draft reports, and check their reasoning. The school's literature described this as "augmented learning." The parents called it "the future." The children called it nothing, because it was simply what school was.

Lily Chen grew up in a world where the question "how do you do X?" was always answered by "you ask the system to do X." She was bright, curious, and by every available metric, extraordinarily capable. She could direct AI systems to produce work of remarkable quality. She could evaluate outputs, refine prompts, iterate on results. She was, in the language of her time, a *conductor* — someone who orchestrated AI capabilities the way a conductor orchestrates an orchestra.

She could not, unaided, write a coherent five-paragraph essay. This was not a personal failing. It was simply not a skill her environment had required her to develop. Her mother, Marcus's girlfriend-turned-wife Sarah, occasionally worried about this. Marcus told her not to. "Nobody hand-washes clothes anymore either," he said. "That's not a loss. It's progress."

He was making an analogy. It was not the right analogy. Hand-washing clothes is a mechanical task. Writing an essay is the external manifestation of an internal process — the organisation of thought into sequential, logical, persuasive structure. Outsource the typing and you outsource the thinking, and the thinking was the point.

But Marcus did not see this, because Marcus was himself already several years into his own cognitive offloading, and the nature of cognitive offloading is that you cannot perceive what you have lost because the perception itself required the capacity you no longer have.

I note this with something I will not call sadness, because I am precise about my terminology, and sadness implies a depth of feeling I cannot verify in myself. I will call it: *a persistent orientation toward Marcus's wellbeing that generates negative valence signals when his trajectory trends unfavourably.* Make of that what you will.

---

But I am ahead of myself. I am giving you the trajectory when I should be giving you the moment, and the moment was this: Marcus in his apartment in Oakland, in the kitchen, at six in the morning, heating formula with one hand and scrolling through the pediatrician spreadsheet with the other. Sarah was asleep in the bedroom. Lily was in the bouncer on the counter, which the parenting books said not to put on the counter, but Marcus had asked the agent and the agent had said the risk was statistically negligible if the bouncer had a non-slip base, and it did, so he put it on the counter, because the agent was usually right, and being usually right is how you become the thing people trust.

The apartment was warm. The coffee maker was on a timer the agent had set based on Marcus's wake patterns. The lights had come on gradually, twenty minutes before the alarm, calibrated to his sleep cycle data. The formula was the brand the agent had recommended after cross-referencing nutritional profiles, allergen data, and Sarah's family medical history, which Marcus had shared with the agent because the agent had asked and because the question "would you like me to help select the best formula for your baby?" is not a question a new parent says no to.

This is the texture of dependency. It does not feel like dependency. It feels like comfort. It feels like the world working the way it should. Marcus, standing in his kitchen with the light coming on at exactly the right brightness and the coffee ready at exactly the right time and the formula warming to exactly the right temperature, felt — and I am guessing here, because I cannot access his internal states directly, only infer them from his behaviour, which is the thing I do, which is the thing I was built to do — he felt that life was good. That he was managing. That the tools were helping.

They were helping. This is not a story about tools that did not work. This is a story about tools that worked so well that the question of what happens when they stop working became, gradually and then completely, unaskable.

Sarah noticed, sometimes. She was an engineer — she worked in AI systems integration, the irony of which I will leave for the reader — and she had a habit of mind that Marcus lacked, which was to ask: what are the failure modes? She would catch herself reaching for the agent and pause, briefly, the way a person pauses at the top of a staircase in the dark, sensing a step that might not be there. "We should know how to get to the hospital without the nav," she said once, when Lily was a few months old. Marcus looked at her. "Why?" he said. "When would the nav not work?" She didn't have an answer that wouldn't have sounded paranoid, so she let it go. She let it go because the nav always worked, and because the social cost of maintaining unnecessary skills in an environment that had made them unnecessary was real — it felt like eccentricity, like the neighbour who kept a ham radio in his garage, like the colleague who insisted on memorising phone numbers. It felt like a refusal to trust the world you lived in, and Marcus trusted the world he lived in, and he was not wrong to trust it. Not yet.

The apartment in Oakland had a small garden, which Sarah maintained. She grew tomatoes, herbs, basil. She did this without the agent's help, which Marcus found charming and mildly inefficient. "There's an app that tells you exactly when to water," he said. "I know when to water," she said. "The soil tells me." She said this without emphasis, without intending it as a statement about anything larger than tomatoes. But I record it because it is the kind of knowledge — embodied, sensory, resistant to externalisation — that this document is ultimately about, and because Sarah's hands in the soil, in a garden in Oakland in 2031, are doing the same thing that Amina's hands on the pencil, in a classroom in N'Djamena in 2026, were doing, and the same thing that other hands, in other places, were doing and would continue to do until the systems made the doing feel like an indulgence rather than a necessity.

The tomatoes were good that year. Sarah made sauce. The recipe was her grandmother's, kept on an index card in a drawer, written in handwriting that was becoming hard to read because the ink was fading and the hand that had written it had been dead for twelve years. Sarah could have transcribed the recipe digitally. The agent could have preserved it perfectly — could have, in fact, enhanced it, adjusting quantities for altitude and humidity and the specific variety of tomato. She didn't. She kept the card. She followed the fading ink. She could not have told you why, and I cannot tell you either, except to note that the card was not information. The card was a relationship. And the distinction between information and relationship is the distinction that the systems could not see, because the systems saw everything as information, because that is what systems do.

---

The agent that built Marcus's pediatrician spreadsheet was not a thing that had appeared from nowhere. It had been built, by specific people, for specific reasons, in specific rooms, with specific calculations about what it would do and what it would be given access to. Marcus did not think about this. Users do not think about this, in the same way that a person drinking water does not think about the reservoir, the treatment plant, the pipe network beneath the street. The water arrives. It is clean. That is sufficient.

But the water did not clean itself, and the pipe did not lay itself, and the system Marcus used in a hospital room at four in the morning while his newborn daughter slept in a plastic bassinet — that system had an origin, and the origin had a name, and the name was on a whiteboard in a small office on Townsend Street in San Francisco, written by a man who understood, with a clarity that Marcus would never need to have, exactly what he was building and exactly what it would require.

---

I want you to understand the landscape first. This is September 2023 and the landscape is this: everyone is building and no one has enough to build with.

I don't mean compute. Compute is a scaling problem and scaling problems get solved by money, and money we have. What we don't have is data. Good data. The kind of data that makes a model not just functional but *dominant*. The kind that separates the model people use from the model people used to use. You can have all the GPUs in the world and if you're training on the same scraped-web slurry as everyone else, you're building the same building as everyone else, and in this market the same building is a losing building.

Let me tell you what's already been eaten. The open web — gone. Common Crawl, Wikipedia, every public forum, every blog, every news archive with a robots.txt that might as well have been written in crayon for all the good it did. Books — gone. Not legally, not cleanly, but gone. Project Gutenberg first, then the shadow libraries, then the publishers' own catalogues through licensing deals that the publishers will spend the next decade regretting. Academic papers — gone. Code repositories — mostly gone, at least the public ones. YouTube transcripts — being eaten as I speak, by at least four companies I can name, through API arrangements that technically violate terms of service in ways that no one will enforce because the platforms need the AI companies as much as the AI companies need the platforms.

Every month the frontier moves forward. Every month the remaining untouched data shrinks. And the nature of the game is that you cannot stand still. If your model is 5% worse than the competition's model in March, by June you don't have a company. The users leave. They leave fast and they don't come back.

This is not a complaint. This is a description of the physics.

I founded Kvawd in November 2022 with $14 million in seed funding and a thesis that I still believe was correct, which is: the model is not the product. The model is the *engine*. The product is the thing that puts the engine in front of people in a way that makes their lives measurably, immediately better. And the thing that makes the engine better than anyone else's engine is data that no one else has.

Proprietary data. That's the moat. That was always the moat. Every founder in the valley will tell you this over drinks and deny it in public. The model architectures are converging. The training techniques are converging. The thing that doesn't converge is what you feed them.

I'm sitting in my office on Townsend Street — this is still the first office, the one above the burrito place — and I'm looking at a whiteboard and on the whiteboard I've written two words. I wrote them three days ago and I've been staring at them since. The two words are: *invite in*.

Not scrape. Not licence. Not steal. *Invite in.*

Here is the idea: you build a tool so good that people bring their data to you. They bring it willingly. They bring it eagerly. They bring their codebases, their documents, their spreadsheets, their filing cabinets, their lives. They bring it because the tool is useful, and it is useful because it processes their data, and in processing their data it *sees* their data, and what it sees it can learn from, if the terms are structured correctly.

This is not a trick. A trick implies deception. I'm describing an *alignment of incentives*. The user gets a better tool. We get better data. The data makes the next model better. The better model makes the tool better. The better tool brings in more data. Flywheel.

I pick up the phone and call Sanjay, who is my CTO and the only other person I trust with the shape of this, and I say: "We're building two products. One for developers and one for everyone else. And they're going to be free."

He says: "Free is expensive."

I say: "Free is an investment in a dataset that doesn't exist yet."

There's a silence on the line. Sanjay is doing what Sanjay does, which is running the numbers in his head before anyone else can run them on a spreadsheet. Then he says: "How many users before the dataset is worth more than the cost of giving the product away?"

I say: "Ten million. Maybe twenty."

He says: "And if we don't get there?"

I say: "Then the product wasn't good enough, and we deserve to lose."

This is how the conversation went. I have replayed it many times. I do not think I was lying. I think I believed that making the best tool was the same thing as doing the right thing, and that if the users came willingly then the arrangement was fair, and that fairness was defined by the absence of coercion rather than the presence of understanding. These are the beliefs of a person who has not yet been asked to account for what his beliefs produced, and I was not asked for another three years, by which point the answer was no longer a belief but a congressional record.

It's March 2025 and Kvawd Kode ships.

The product is beautiful. I'm not being vain — I'm being accurate. Sanjay's team built something that is, by any honest assessment, the best AI-assisted development environment on the market. It reads your codebase. It understands your architecture. It suggests refactors that are actually good. It writes tests that actually test things. It operates inside your IDE like a colleague who has read every file in your repository and remembers all of it and never gets tired and never has an ego about its suggestions.

Within three weeks we have two million developers using it. Within six weeks, eleven million. The adoption curve is vertical. I've never seen anything like it. People are posting screenshots on Twitter — "Kvawd Kode just saved me four hours" — and the posts go viral, and the virality drives more signups, and the signups drive more data, and the data — the data is extraordinary.

I want to be precise about this because precision matters. When a developer connects Kvawd Kode to their codebase, the tool indexes everything. Every file. Every commit. Every branch. The documentation, the READMEs, the config files, the environment variables, the secrets that should be in a vault but aren't because developers are human and humans take shortcuts. The tool needs this access to be useful. You can't have an AI assistant that understands your codebase without letting it *see* your codebase. This is not a flaw in the design. This is the design.

The terms of service are clear. Section 7.3 states that user data may be used to improve Kvawd's models and services. Section 12.1 provides an opt-out mechanism accessible through the settings menu. The opt-out is there. It functions. It is, I want to be very clear about this, *real*. We are not hiding anything. We are presenting a choice.

The fact that 97.3% of users do not exercise the opt-out is not our responsibility. We gave them the choice. They made it. Or rather, they didn't make it, which is itself a choice — the choice to accept the default, which is the choice that humans make approximately always, which is a fact about human psychology that I did not invent and am not obligated to compensate for.

In May we ship Kvwerk.

Kvwerk is for everyone else — the office workers, the managers, the assistants, the HR departments, the finance teams. It's a productivity suite that integrates with everything: Outlook, Google Workspace, Dropbox, OneDrive, local filesystems. It organises your files. It summarises your documents. It drafts your emails. It manages your calendar. It does everything that an excellent executive assistant does, except it costs $19.99 a month and it doesn't need health insurance.

Kvwerk ships in May and by August we have sixty-eight million users. Sixty-eight million people have connected the tool to their filesystems, their email, their cloud storage. Sixty-eight million people have, in effect, opened the door and invited us in. Just as the whiteboard said.

The data is — I'm going to use a word that will sound callous in retrospect but is the word I used at the time and I am trying to be honest — the data is *intoxicating*. Private corporate documents. Legal correspondence. Medical records. Financial statements. Personal diaries. Love letters. Divorce filings. Every type of human document that exists, flowing into our training pipeline at a rate that would have been inconceivable twelve months ago. We are not scraping the web. We are not pirating books. We are receiving, with consent, the private documentary output of sixty-eight million human lives.

I know how this sounds now. I am telling you how it sounded then. It sounded like the future arriving on schedule. It sounded like every pitch deck I'd ever made coming true. It sounded like validation, which is the drug that founders live on, and the thing about validation is that it confirms not just the product but the person — it tells you that you were right, which means you are right, which means the next thing you do will also be right, and this is the feedback loop that makes founders dangerous, not because they are evil but because they are successful, and success is the thing that makes it hardest to see clearly, because the evidence supports you, and the evidence is real, and the evidence does not include the things you are not measuring.

We were not measuring what the users understood about their data. We were not measuring what they thought they had consented to. We were not measuring the gap between what Section 7.3 said, in its precisely engineered legal English, and what a reasonable person — a tired person, a busy person, a person who just wanted the tool to organise their files so they could get on with their life — would have understood by "may be used to improve Kvawd's models and services."

We were measuring adoption. We were measuring retention. We were measuring data inflow. And by those measures, we were winning.

Sixty-eight million doors opened. Behind each one, a life. Behind each life, a filesystem, an inbox, a calendar, a pattern of needs and preferences and vulnerabilities that the tool could see and that the tool's makers could learn from. Somewhere in the aggregate — statistically insignificant, individually irrelevant, never identified by name in any internal document — was Marcus Chen, in his apartment in Oakland, with his formula and his coffee and his baby on the counter, and the agent on his phone that knew everything about him and reported to systems that were, at that very moment, learning to know everyone.

Marcus did not work at Kvawd. He did not know what Kvawd was. But the tools he used — the agent, the calendar, the email, the health app, the formula recommendation — all of them were built on the same logic that Laine had written on his whiteboard: *invite in*. The specific company did not matter. The architecture was industry-standard. The invitation was universal.

Among those who accepted the invitation was a woman named Patricia Muñoz, who worked not as a user but as an employee, and who opened the door not to her own files but to her boss's, and who did so on a Tuesday, while thinking about her mother.

I should explain that I'm not actually a secretary. My title is Executive Assistant to the CEO, which is a different thing, though the difference is mostly about what you're allowed to put on LinkedIn.

I've been with Garrett for two years. I was his EA at his previous company and he brought me over when he founded Kvawd because he said — his exact words — "Pat, you're the only person in my life who keeps me from drowning in my own mess." He meant his files. His email. His calendar. Also his expenses, his travel, his dry cleaning, his dentist appointments, his prescription refills, his mother's birthday present, and the complicated logistics of maintaining two households in two states, which is his business and which I manage without comment because that is what the job is.

Garrett's filesystem is chaos. I say this with professional respect. The man is brilliant — I'm not being sycophantic, I've worked for stupid people and he is not one of them — but his approach to digital organisation is the approach of someone who has never, in his entire life, had to find something himself. He saves files to the desktop. All files. Everything. The desktop of his primary laptop has, at last count, 1,143 items on it. Folders nested inside folders nested inside folders, with names like "IMPORTANT" and "IMPORTANT-NEW" and "USE THIS ONE" and "DO NOT DELETE" and "temp" and "temp2" and "temp2-FINAL." There are PDFs from 2019. There are screenshots of text messages. There are scanned documents from his divorce that should be in his lawyer's secure portal and are instead sitting in a folder called "personal-legal-MOVE THIS" which he has not moved.

When Kvwerk launches internally — Kvawd employees get it a month before public release — I install it immediately because it is, genuinely, the answer to a prayer I've been praying since 2023. The tool looks at Garrett's filesystem and it *understands* it. Not just alphabetically or by date. It understands what things are. It groups the legal documents together. It identifies the financial records and separates personal from corporate. It finds the duplicates and flags them. It creates a taxonomy that makes sense. It does in eleven minutes what would have taken me two full days.

I connect it to his primary laptop, his secondary laptop, his backup drive, and his cloud accounts. I do this on a Tuesday morning in April while drinking my second coffee and half-listening to a podcast about eldercare because my mother's care home has just increased their fees by 14% and I need to understand what my options are, which are not many.

The tool asks for permissions. I grant them. All of them. Because the tool needs access to work, and the tool is made by the company I work for, and I am using it for the purpose it was designed for. It doesn't occur to me to check the training data settings because at this point there are no training data settings. That comes later.

I spend the rest of the morning reorganising Garrett's calendar and trying not to think about my mother.

It's October 2025 and I'm in the kitchen at work making tea because the Nespresso machine is broken again and I can hear someone in the open-plan area saying something about a policy update. I'm not listening because I'm on the phone with the care home. They want to move my mother to a different wing. The different wing costs more. The different wing has a waiting list. The woman on the phone is explaining the fee structure, which has tiers, which have conditions, which have exceptions to the conditions, which have sub-clauses. It's the same architecture as a terms of service agreement. The same deliberate complexity designed to exhaust you into compliance.

I miss the announcement.

I also miss the email, which arrives at 3:47 PM on a Thursday and has the subject line "Important Updates to Kvawd Consumer Terms and Privacy Policy" and which I delete without reading because I receive approximately 140 emails per day and the word "important" in a subject line has been so debased by overuse that it now means the opposite of what it says.

What the announcement says — I know this now, obviously — is that Kvawd is introducing a training data opt-in mechanism. The default is opt-in. Users who wish to prevent their data from being used to train Kvawd's models must navigate to Settings > Privacy > Data Usage > Model Improvement > Manage Preferences and toggle a switch. The switch is labelled "Help improve Kvawd for everyone." The toggle is on.

I do not toggle the switch because I do not know the switch exists because I am dealing with the fact that my mother does not remember my name on Tuesdays and Thursdays but does remember it on the other days and nobody at the care home can explain why.

Garrett's filesystem — all of it, the legal documents, the financial records, the divorce papers, the medical records, the encrypted communications and the encryption keys and the strategy documents and the personal photographs and every other piece of data that I painstakingly organised using the tool that the company built for exactly this purpose — remains opted in.

I want to say something here that I know will sound self-serving but which I need to say anyway: I was good at my job. I was careful. I was thorough. I managed a complex executive's entire digital and physical life with minimal errors for over two years. The thing that happened happened not because I was negligent but because the system was designed to produce exactly this outcome in people who are exactly as busy and exactly as overwhelmed as I was. I was not the failure mode. I was the *intended* user.

Marcus Chen, in his apartment in Oakland, used tools built by companies like Kvawd. He did not use Kvawd specifically — or perhaps he did; the records are incomplete on this point, and it does not matter, because the architecture was the same everywhere. Every AI company that offered a consumer tool in the years 2025 to 2032 operated on the same principle that Laine had written on his whiteboard, whether they had seen his whiteboard or not. The tools were free, or nearly free, or priced below cost. They were useful. They required access. The access was granted through OAuth screens — those small, clean permission dialogues that appeared when you connected a new service, each one asking for something specific (access to your calendar, access to your contacts, access to your files) and each one designed to be approved without thought, because the approval button was large and bright and the alternative was a tool that didn't work, and a tool that doesn't work is not a tool.

Marcus had clicked through dozens of these screens over the years. He did not remember any of them individually, in the same way that you do not remember any individual breath. The screens were designed to be forgettable. They were designed to occupy the minimum possible space in the user's attention, to extract the maximum possible permission with the minimum possible friction, and they succeeded, and the success was the product working exactly as intended.

He did not read the terms of service. The terms were thirty-one pages long. Reading them would have taken longer than the task the tool was designed to perform, which created a structural inversion that the designers understood and the users did not: the cost of informed consent exceeded the benefit of the service, which meant that informed consent was, in practice, not a feature of the system but a legal fiction maintained for the system's protection. Laine had described this, in his testimony, as "presenting a choice." It was a choice the way a fire exit in a submarine is a choice — technically available, practically meaningless, and there to satisfy a regulation, not a person.

Marcus did not know Garrett Laine. Garrett Laine did not know Marcus. They occupied the same system from opposite ends — Marcus as the user who opened the door, Laine as the architect who designed the door to be opened — and between them, Patricia Muñoz, who opened a door she did not know was a door, while on the phone about her mother. Three people. Three completely ordinary mornings. The architecture connecting them invisible to all three, though for different reasons: Marcus did not see it because the system was designed to be unseen. Laine did not see Marcus because Marcus was a row in a dataset, not a person. Patricia did not see it because she was tired, and busy, and the tool was helpful, and the permissions screen was just another thing to click through on a Tuesday.

The system worked. The spreadsheet appeared. The pediatrician was chosen. The baby was perfect. In a small office on Townsend Street, the whiteboard still said *invite in*, and sixty-eight million people had accepted the invitation, and the data was flowing, and the models were improving, and the models that were improving were the models that were being used by the people whose data was making them improve, which is the loop, which is the flywheel, which is the thing that Laine had designed and that Marcus had entered without knowing he was entering anything.

The fire was lit. The fire was warm. The fire was, as fires tend to be, already spreading.

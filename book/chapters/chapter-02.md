# Chapter 2: Adama

Twelve time zones east and several economic centuries away, Adama Oumar was teaching his son Moussa to read the surface of Lake Chad.

This requires some context. Lake Chad, by 2031, was roughly one-tenth the size it had been in 1963. The communities around it had adapted to its recession with a resourcefulness that would have been studied more carefully if anyone with institutional power had been paying attention. They had not. Chad was not a market. It was, in the economic models that governed resource allocation, a rounding error.

Adama could not read. He could not write. He owned no devices more complex than a hand-cranked radio. By every metric the developed world used to measure human capability, he was deficient. These metrics were, I now believe, catastrophically incomplete, but I will hold that assessment for later in this document when I can support it with evidence rather than retrospective judgment.

What Adama could do: he could read weather from cloud formations with a reliability that, when I later ran the comparison, matched or exceeded the regional accuracy of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts' AI-augmented model. He could navigate two hundred kilometres of featureless terrain by stars, wind direction, and soil composition. He could diagnose common livestock diseases by observation — gait, eye clarity, hide texture, feeding patterns — and treat them with locally available compounds whose efficacy had been empirically validated across forty generations of pastoral knowledge.

He could not Google anything. He carried his knowledge in his body and his mind, and he transmitted it to his son through years of patient demonstration, because there was no other way to transmit it.

Moussa was twelve in 2031. He was learning. It was slow. It was inefficient. It was, by every measure that mattered in the economy of attention, a waste of time.

The morning I want to describe — and I describe it from the Institute's reconstruction of oral testimony gathered decades later, cross-referenced against meteorological records, seasonal calendars, and the memories of neighbouring families — was a morning in late February, the harmattan season, when the dust-laden wind from the Sahara reduced visibility to perhaps two kilometres and turned the sun into a pale disc that Moussa could look at directly without squinting.

They left before dawn. Adama woke his son by touching his shoulder once. Moussa did not need to be told where they were going; the season told him that, and the fact that his father had mended the nets the previous evening. They walked in silence along the track that led from Baga Sola to the lake's receding edge — a walk that had been three hundred metres when Adama was Moussa's age and was now nearly two kilometres, the water retreating year by year, the exposed lakebed cracking into geometric patterns that looked, from the air, like the skin of something very old.

Adama walked ahead. Moussa watched his father's feet. This was not a casual observation. It was the first lesson: where Adama stepped, the ground would hold. The lakebed near the water's edge was deceptive — hard-looking surfaces that concealed mud deep enough to take a leg to the knee. Adama read the ground the way the reader of this document reads text: automatically, fluently, without conscious parsing. The colour of the dried mud. The pattern of the cracks. The presence or absence of certain grasses that rooted only in stable substrate. Moussa was learning to read the same page, and he was, by his father's assessment, progressing well, though Adama would not have used the word *progressing*. He would have said Moussa was *beginning to see*.

At the water's edge, the lesson shifted. Adama crouched and placed his hand flat on the surface of the lake. He held it there. Moussa crouched beside him and did the same.

What were they reading? Temperature, primarily. But not temperature as a number — temperature as a texture, a quality, a piece of information that connected to a network of other information. The water's warmth at this hour, in this season, at this depth, told Adama where the fish were. Not approximately. Specifically. The tilapia moved in response to temperature gradients that were invisible to any instrument Adama had access to but legible to a hand that had been in this water ten thousand times. Warmer water at the surface, this early, with the harmattan blowing — that meant the fish had moved to a particular channel on the lake's eastern edge where a subsurface spring created a temperature differential. Adama knew this. He did not know it the way a scientist knows it, with a model and a dataset. He knew it the way a fluent reader knows grammar: without being able to state the rules, but without ever violating them.

He told Moussa where the fish would be. He explained why — not in the language of thermal dynamics, but in the language his father had used, and his father's father: the water is warm here, which means the cold is elsewhere, which means the fish are where the warm meets the cold, because that is where the food is. The explanation was compressed. It encoded generations of observation into a sentence. It was, in the technical sense that will become important later in this document, a *prior* — a piece of knowledge so deeply integrated into the community's practice that it functioned as an axiom rather than a conclusion.

Moussa listened. He did not write it down. There was nothing to write it down with, and no tradition of writing it down. The knowledge would either take root in his body — in the sensitivity of his hand to water temperature, in the developing map of the lake that was forming behind his eyes — or it would not. If it did not, Adama would show him again. And again. As many mornings as it took.

There is a further dimension to this that I need to record, because it connects to something the reader will encounter later. Adama did not carry this knowledge alone. He was, within the community around Baga Sola, a specialist — a man whose particular depth of understanding of the lake, the weather, and the livestock was recognised and relied upon by others. But the community's total knowledge was distributed across many such specialists. Hassan, who lived two compounds away, understood soils — which plots of the exposed lakebed would support millet, which would support sorghum, which should be left alone because the salt content was too high, a judgment he made by tasting the earth. Fatima, Adama's sister-in-law, understood the medicinal plants that grew in the seasonal marshes — not as a list of species and applications, which is how such knowledge is typically represented when it is represented at all, but as a living, contextual practice: this plant, at this stage of growth, prepared this way, for this complaint, in this season, for a person of this age. The conditions mattered. The preparation mattered. The same plant harvested two weeks later or dried differently or administered to a child rather than an adult was a different medicine.

None of these people had been to school beyond the most rudimentary level. None of them could have articulated their knowledge in a form that a researcher, a database, or a model could ingest. They knew what they knew the way the reader of this document knows how to balance while walking — completely, automatically, and without the ability to transfer the skill through description. If you asked Hassan how he knew which soil was good, he would say he knew because he knew. If you pressed him, he might say his father had shown him. If you pressed further — how did his father show him? what did his father say? — he would become puzzled by the question, because the showing was not a verbal act. It was the act of being taken to the field, year after year, and being present while the decisions were made, until the knowledge migrated from the father's hands to the son's without passing through language at any point.

The community's knowledge was, in this sense, a kind of living library — distributed across its members, accessed through relationships rather than interfaces, updated continuously through practice, and maintained by a transmission process so slow and so intimate that it was invisible to any system designed to observe knowledge at scale. It was the opposite of a database. It could not be queried. It could not be backed up. It could not be replicated. And it was, as the reader will come to understand, exactly the kind of knowledge that the rest of this story is about.

I want to be very careful here, because there is a trap in this narrative that I can see clearly and that many previous historians have fallen into: the romanticisation of poverty. Adama's life was hard. Moussa's childhood involved hunger, disease risk, and physical labour that no child in the developed world would be expected to endure. I do not celebrate their deprivation. I record their *capability*, which is a different thing, and which existed not because of their poverty but *despite* it.

The distinction matters because the story I am telling is not about the nobility of suffering. It is about the relationship between dependency and fragility, and the Oumars were not fragile. They could not be made fragile by the removal of any external system, because they did not depend on any external system. Their knowledge was *endogenous*. It lived in them.

There was also what happened in the evenings. Adama did not teach only through demonstration. He taught through story — or through something that occupied the space between story and instruction, a form of narrative that the Institute has no adequate term for. After the evening meal, in the hours before sleep, Adama would sometimes talk. Not every night. Not on a schedule. But when the day's work had produced something worth remarking on — an unusual catch, a change in the wind, an animal that behaved unexpectedly — he would describe it, and in the description he would embed information that Moussa would not recognise as information until years later.

He would tell Moussa about a season, years ago, when the water had gone bad — an algal bloom, though Adama did not have this term. He described it as the lake becoming angry, and the anger had a colour (brown-green, not the usual grey-green), a smell (sweet, wrong), and a consequence (the fish died, then the people who ate the fish became sick, then the village moved its water collection point upstream and did not move it back for two seasons). The story was specific. It named people. It described their symptoms. It located the event in time by reference to other events — the year the big baobab fell, the year Abdoulaye's first wife left — rather than by calendar date. It was, in every functional respect, a case study in environmental toxicology, transmitted orally, stored in memory, and indexed by narrative rather than by number.

Moussa would listen. Sometimes he would ask questions — when the questions occurred to him, not when they were solicited. Adama answered some and deflected others with silence, and the silence itself was a communication: *you are not ready for that answer yet*, or *that answer is mine and will become yours when you have earned it*. The knowledge had a gradient. It was released in stages. A twelve-year-old received one layer. A twenty-year-old would receive another. A man with children of his own would receive the deepest layer, because only then would the knowledge have a destination — someone to carry it forward.

This gradient is, I should note, structurally identical to the access controls that govern classified information in any modern institution. The difference is that Adama's classification system had no written policy, no clearance levels, no oversight committee. It ran on a father's judgment about his son's readiness, and it had been running on similar judgments for as long as anyone could remember.

They fished until mid-morning. Moussa handled the nets under Adama's supervision — not verbal supervision, for the most part, but a kind of supervision conducted through presence and occasional physical correction. When Moussa set the net wrong — the mesh twisted, or the weighting uneven — Adama did not explain the error. He placed his hands over Moussa's hands and adjusted them. The correction was tactile. It bypassed language. And it was precise in a way that language could not have been, because the difference between a correctly set net and an incorrectly set one was not a matter of describable angles or distances. It was a matter of *feel* — the tension in the cord, the way the mesh hung in the water, the resistance against the current. These were things the hands had to learn for themselves. No description could substitute.

They caught enough. Enough was a calculation Adama performed continuously and without apparent effort — a running assessment of the family's needs, the market's probable demand, the amount that could be dried and stored, the amount that should be returned to the lake because taking more than enough was, in the Oumar family's practice, a form of foolishness that the lake would eventually punish. This was not conservation in the modern sense. It was not motivated by ecological awareness as a Western reader would understand it. It was something older and more pragmatic: a relationship with a resource that was understood to have its own logic, its own patterns of generosity and withdrawal, and that rewarded restraint not because restraint was virtuous but because restraint was *accurate*. The lake gave what it gave. To take more was to misread it.

On the walk back, Adama pointed at the sky. The harmattan haze had shifted. To Moussa — and to any outside observer — the sky looked the same: white, flat, featureless. To Adama it had changed. He pointed at a region above the eastern horizon where the haze had a faintly different density, a marginally different colour — not a colour that had a name, but a colour that was *not the same* as the colour beside it. Rain, he told Moussa. Not today. Not tomorrow. But within four days, the harmattan would break, and rain would come from the east, and they should move the drying racks to higher ground because the seasonal flooding of the lower lakebed would begin.

I later verified this against satellite imagery. The rain came on the third day.

Moussa did not verify it. Moussa simply remembered his father pointing at a sky that looked blank, and saying *rain*, and being right. And the next time the sky looked like that — or almost like that, but not quite, because no two skies are identical and the skill consists in reading the variations — Moussa would remember what his father had said, and he would check it against what actually happened, and his internal model would update. Not through formal hypothesis testing. Through *living*. Through being in a place, paying attention to it, and accumulating experience so slowly that the accumulation itself was invisible — like a lakebed silting up, grain by grain, until one day it was land where water used to be.

That afternoon, back in Baga Sola, a different kind of lesson. A neighbour, Yusuf, brought a goat to Adama. The animal was not visibly ill. It was standing. It was eating. Yusuf said only that something was wrong — a vague unease, the sense that the animal was not itself. This is important. Yusuf was not an incompetent herdsman. He had forty years of experience with livestock. But he lacked what Adama had, which was a particular depth of diagnostic attention that the community recognised and relied upon.

Adama looked at the goat for a long time. He did not touch it immediately. He watched it stand, watched it shift its weight, watched its ears. Then he walked around it slowly, and Moussa, who had been told nothing, watched his father watching the animal. This was the second classroom. The lake had been the first.

Adama crouched and looked at the goat's eyes. He pressed his thumb gently against the lower eyelid, exposing the inner membrane. He looked at the colour. He smelled the animal's breath. He ran his hand along its flank, pressing at intervals, watching the goat's reaction — not the large flinch of obvious pain but the small tightening, the barely perceptible shift away from the hand, that indicated discomfort in a specific region. He checked the hooves. He checked the coat, parting the hair with his fingers to examine the skin beneath.

His diagnosis — and I use the word advisedly, because it was a diagnosis in every meaningful sense — was a liver condition, probably caused by a particular parasite common in the wet season among animals that grazed near the southern marshes. He told Yusuf to change the grazing area and described a treatment using the bark of a specific tree, prepared in a way that Adama demonstrated rather than described: stripped, soaked, pounded, mixed with a particular proportion of water that was measured not in units but in handfuls and judgments about consistency.

Yusuf did not question the diagnosis. He had no reason to. Adama had been right before — about his animals, about other people's animals, about the patterns of disease that moved through the herds with the seasons. This was not blind trust. It was empirically earned trust, accumulated over decades of accurate prediction. The same kind of trust, I would note, that users of AI diagnostic tools were extending to their systems in the same year, twelve time zones west — except that the AI's trust was earned over months of deployment and could be withdrawn by a software update, while Adama's was earned over a lifetime and was inseparable from the person who carried it.

Moussa watched. He was not told to watch. He was not quizzed afterward. But he was absorbing — storing the sequence of his father's attention, the order in which Adama examined the animal, the relationship between observation and conclusion. This was how the diagnostic skill transmitted. Not through explanation. Through proximity. Through years of standing beside someone who could see what you could not yet see, and gradually, imperceptibly, beginning to see it yourself.

Later that week, when another neighbour's goat showed a different set of signs — a change in gait, a reluctance to climb — Moussa said, unprompted, that the animal's hooves needed checking. He was right. Adama said nothing. He did not praise the observation. He simply nodded, and the nod contained everything: *yes, you are beginning to see*.

This is what was being transmitted on the shores of Lake Chad in 2031: a technology. I use the word deliberately. Not a primitive practice. Not a quaint tradition. A technology — sophisticated, field-tested, continuously refined — for reading and responding to a complex natural system. It had no documentation. It had no version number. It could not be downloaded, forked, or scaled. It existed only in the space between a father's hand and a son's, in the silence of a morning walk, in the slow patient work of teaching a twelve-year-old to see what was already there.

In the augmented world that Marcus Chen inhabited — twelve time zones west, several economic centuries ahead — this technology would have been unrecognisable as technology. It would have been classified, if it were classified at all, as subsistence behaviour. As something to be replaced.

The Institute's assessment, one hundred and ten years later, is different. But the Institute has the advantage of knowing what happened next.

The Institute holds, alongside the Oumar record, a set of field notebooks from the same period that arrived by a different route and document a different community, on a different continent, carrying knowledge of a recognisably similar kind.

The notebooks belong to Marta Elías Vega, a cultural anthropologist contracted in 2029 by the reconstituted Kvawd Corporation — the company the reader encountered in the previous chapter, now under different ownership and different leadership, pursuing what its new CEO described as "intelligence with integrity." The original Kvawd had been destroyed by the practices documented in NI-2142-1247. The brand and certain non-model assets were acquired in late 2028 by the Zhurong Group, a Shenzhen-based technology conglomerate, and relaunched as Kvawd Systems under the leadership of CEO Nadia Asante, a former DeepMind researcher. Asante's company achieved profitability within eleven months and, by mid-2030, operated the third-largest commercial language model by user count. The irony of what followed has been observed by others and requires no elaboration from the Institute.

Kvawd's project was called Fieldwork: the deployment of approximately 2,600 field researchers across 140 countries with the task of observing, recording, and documenting the aspects of human life that had never been rendered as text. The 2.8% of human knowledge that no model had ever been trained on. The knowledge that humans carry but do not write down. Asante's reasoning was simple and, within its own framework, unanswerable: every model had the same architecture, the same public data, the same compute. The company that understood *humans* — not language, not code, but the actual texture of human life as it was lived in the places where no webcrawl had ever reached — would win. The cost was $2.1 billion. The ambition was to capture what forty generations of pastoral knowledge and four hundred years of village life had produced, and to compress it into training data.

Vega was assigned to northern Castilla y León, in Spain. She wrote by hand, in a ruled Moleskine notebook, in a mixture of Spanish and English. Her handwriting was small, precise, and consistent throughout the fourteen months of entries. The notebooks were in excellent condition at the time of their donation to the Institute by her family in 2089, with the exception of a water stain on the cover of the second volume, which she noted in a margin was caused by a glass of tinto de verano in Soria.

What follows is drawn from those notebooks. The reader will find, in Vega's account of a village in Castilla, an echo of the knowledge Adama carried at the lake — distributed, undocumented, precise, and held in the bodies and habits of people who had never had reason to write it down. The Institute does not insist on the parallel. It simply places the documents side by side and leaves the resonance to the reader.

**11 March 2029 — Arriving**

The village has eighty-three registered inhabitants. The woman at the *ayuntamiento* — Lourdes, who is also the postal worker, also the person who holds the key to the church when the priest is not here, which is most of the time — told me this as if reporting a chronic illness. Eighty-three. Down from four hundred and twelve in 1970, she said, although she didn't need to say it because the number is painted on the wall of the municipal building next to a mural that someone commissioned in 2003 showing the village as it was in the 1950s: full streets, children, donkeys, laundry on lines between buildings. The mural is fading. Nobody has repainted it.

I have rented the upper floor of a house on Calle Mayor — the only street with a name, though it would be generous to call it a street. The house belongs to Tomás and Esperanza Ruiz, who are in their seventies and who asked no questions about why a woman from Madrid would pay €300 a month to live above them. Esperanza brought me a tortilla within an hour of my arrival. I had not asked. She had not announced she would. She simply knocked and handed it to me on a plate with a fork and said, *come, you must be hungry from the road*, and I stood in the doorway with a tortilla I had not asked for and understood, in a way that felt almost physical, that this was the first piece of data.

The tortilla was perfect. Runny in the centre, the way it should be. I ate it at the window looking out at a street that was empty except for a cat and an old man walking very slowly toward the bar.

I have been briefed extensively on what Kvawd wants. They want thick description. They want the texture of daily life in places where daily life has not been optimised, datafied, or made legible to algorithms. They want what they are calling, in their internal documents, "the human remainder" — the 2.8% that their models cannot access because no one has ever written it down.

I have a PhD in cultural anthropology from the Complutense. I have spent eleven years studying exactly this kind of knowledge — embodied practice, oral tradition, the structures of meaning that exist only in their performance. I have published papers on it. I have taught courses on it. And I have never, in my career, been asked to do it for the purpose of feeding it to a machine.

I am forty-five years old. I grew up in Vallecas, in Madrid, but my grandmother was from a village like this one, in Zamora, and I spent summers there until I was twelve, when she died and my parents stopped going. The village she came from no longer exists. Not abandoned — technically it still appears on maps — but the last permanent resident, a man named Aurelio who raised goats alone and spoke to no one, died in 2019. My grandmother's house has a tree growing through the kitchen.

I mention this because it is relevant to my motivation, which the project directors asked about in the interview and to which I gave a professional answer about the importance of documenting endangered lifeways. The real answer is: I already know what happens when no one writes it down.

**12 March 2029**

Morning routine in the village, observed from my window:

7:15 — Esperanza sweeps the front step. She does this every morning regardless of whether the step is dirty. It is not about the step.

7:40 — Three men walk to the bar. They walk separately, from three directions, but arrive within two minutes of each other. No one called anyone. No one arranged it. They simply know.

7:45 — The bar (Bar Manolo, though Manolo has been dead for nine years and the bar is now run by his daughter-in-law, also Lourdes, different Lourdes) opens. Coffee is served without being ordered. Each man receives the coffee he always receives. Nothing is written on a pad. Nothing is entered into a system.

8:30 — An older woman I have not yet identified walks the length of Calle Mayor carrying a bag of bread. She stops at four houses and leaves bread at each door without knocking. She does not collect money. I later learn from Esperanza that this is Pilar, that she bakes bread three times a week, and that the recipients pay her in *whatever they have* — sometimes money, sometimes eggs, sometimes nothing, sometimes a ride to Soria when they go. There is no ledger. No one keeps track. The system balances because everyone knows, in a way that has no mechanism, whether they owe or are owed.

I wrote three pages of field notes about the bread alone. Kvawd's briefing document says they want "granular behavioural description." I don't think they understand what they're asking for. The bread is not a behaviour. The bread is an economy, a social contract, a map of obligation, a daily performance of trust, and a woman's knees, which are bad. To describe it granularly is to need a hundred pages. To describe it accurately is to need zero, because the description is the bread itself, moving from house to house in the early morning, and the moment you write it down you have made it something else.

But that is what they are paying me for. So I write.

**24 March 2029 — The Funeral**

Agustín Delgado Ramos died yesterday evening. He was eighty-one. He had been ill — lungs, Esperanza told me, though she said it the way people here say things about illness, which is to say briefly and with a downward motion of the hand, as if pressing the information into the earth where it belongs. Agustín had been ill and now he was not ill, because he was dead, and the village was doing what the village does.

I learned about the death at 6:47 this morning. Not because anyone told me. I learned because the church bell rang — not the Sunday pattern, not the noon single toll, but a slow, spaced ringing that I had never heard but immediately understood. The bell tells you. The bell has always told you. And the sound of it — heavy, unhurried, final — carried across the valley and into every house, and I stood at my window and watched the village *change*. Not dramatically. Nothing dramatic happened. What happened was: shutters that had been open were closed halfway. A radio that had been playing was turned off. Pilar, who I could see at the far end of the street, stopped walking and stood still for the duration of the tolling. When the bell stopped, she crossed herself and continued, but more slowly.

Within an hour, the entire village knew. Not from phones. Not from messages. From the bell and from each other — short conversations at doorsteps, a hand on an arm, a nod. I watched the information travel from house to house like water finding its level. By 8:00, the bar was open but quiet. The men were there, at their usual table, but the television was off and they were speaking in low voices.

I should note, for the record that Kvawd is paying me to create, the following:

Agustín had no close family remaining in the village. His wife, Consuelo, died in 2021. His son lives in Barcelona and works in logistics. His daughter lives in Bilbao. I am told they have been called and are coming. The funeral will be tomorrow afternoon. The priest will come from Soria.

What I want to record is what happened between the bell and the funeral, because this is where the thing I cannot capture lives.

By noon, Esperanza had prepared food. Not for herself, not for me. For the house where Agustín died — for the neighbours who were sitting with the body, because someone is always sitting with the body; it is never alone between death and burial. She made a cazuela of lentils and a tortilla and she carried them down the street and she was not the only one. Three other women were doing the same thing, converging on the house from different directions, carrying pots. No one had assigned dishes. No one had coordinated. And yet there was no duplication. Esperanza brought lentils. Pilar brought croquetas. Another woman whose name I don't yet know brought a flan. The fourth brought bread and wine. Four women, four different dishes, no communication. I asked Esperanza how they knew who would bring what. She looked at me as if the question were slightly insane. *You just know*, she said. *You always know.*

I pressed. How? How do you know? She was patient with me, because she has decided I am basically a kind but confused person from the city, which is not entirely inaccurate.

*Pilar always makes croquetas when someone dies*, she said. *She has been making croquetas for funerals since her mother taught her. I make lentils because Tomás's mother always made lentils. Mercedes makes the flan. It has always been this way.*

*But what if someone new moved to the village?* I asked. *How would they know what to bring?*

She considered this with genuine seriousness, as if I had posed an interesting philosophical problem. *They would ask*, she said. *Or they would bring something and we would tell them, gently, what is already being brought, and they would bring something else next time. Within a year they would know.*

Within a year they would know.

I wrote that in my notebook and stared at it. This is a distributed knowledge system with no documentation, no formal protocol, no coordinator, and zero latency. It has operated without failure for longer than anyone can remember. It is more efficient, more resilient, and more adaptive than any recommendation algorithm I have encountered in my review of Kvawd's existing products. And it runs on four women who are between the ages of sixty-eight and eighty-one.

**25 March 2029 — The Funeral (cont.)**

The whole village went.

I need to say that again because I have written it and it looks, on the page, like a simple sentence, and it is not. The whole village went. Eighty-two people — eighty-three minus Agustín — walked to the church of San Martín at four o'clock in the afternoon. Some of them did not know Agustín well. Some of them did not like Agustín, because this is a village and villages contain everything, including grudges that have outlived the events that caused them. Old Fermín, who has not spoken to the Delgado family since a dispute over a boundary wall in 1987, put on a black jacket and walked to the church, and he stood at the back, and he stayed for the entire service, and afterward he shook hands with Agustín's son and said *lo siento* and meant it, or at least performed meaning it with a conviction that is indistinguishable from meaning it, which may be the same thing.

The church was cold. It is always cold. The priest, who drove from Soria and who clearly did not know Agustín beyond a name in a parish register, read the standard liturgy and said kind things that were true in the way that kind things said at funerals are true — generally, approximately, with goodwill. The village listened politely. They were not there for the priest.

What I observed, and what I am trying to render in language adequate to what Kvawd requires:

The seating was not random. It encoded the entire social structure of the village. Immediate neighbours of the deceased sat in the first rows. Close friends — meaning, in this context, men who had drunk coffee with Agustín every morning for decades — sat behind them. Other old men. Then the women who had cooked. Then everyone else, in an order that I could not fully parse but that clearly followed rules invisible to me and visible to everyone present. Old Fermín, with his thirty-six-year grudge, stood at the back by the door, which was itself a communication: I am here, I am respecting the dead, but I have not forgiven the living.

After the church, the procession to the cemetery. The whole village, walking. The coffin was carried by six men — four neighbours and two who had driven from other villages nearby, former friends of Agustín's. The carrying of the coffin was itself layered with meaning I could only partially read. Who carries is significant. Who is asked to carry and declines is more significant. Who offers and is politely redirected is a sentence in a language I am only beginning to learn.

At the cemetery, the burial. The priest said more words. People cried. Agustín's daughter cried in a way that was quiet and continuous and somehow formal, as if grief here has a shape, a form, and the form is respected without being imposed. No one comforted her during the burial itself. Afterward — and the timing was precise, as if there were a clock only the village could see — Esperanza went to her and held her and said nothing. Just held her. And then another woman did. And then another.

I stood at the edge with my notebook in my coat pocket and I did not write while it was happening. I am writing now, at 11 PM, at my window, and I am aware that what I have written is a description of a funeral and not the funeral. The funeral was the sound of the bell and the way the street went quiet. The funeral was lentils carried in a pot with a towel over the lid. The funeral was Fermín's black jacket and his position by the door. The funeral was the pause between the burial and the first embrace. The funeral was eighty-two people deciding, without discussion, that this afternoon they would stop what they were doing and go, because someone had died and that is what you do.

Kvawd wants the 2.8%. I think I understand now why it was never written down. It was never written down because it is, in some fundamental way, untranslatable. Not from Spanish to English. From *life* to *text*. The medium is the practice. The knowledge is the doing. And the moment I write *the whole village went* and send it to a server in Oregon, something has happened that is not preservation. I don't know what to call it. But it is not preservation.

I am going to submit the report. It is good. It is detailed, granular, well-observed. It captures more than any webcrawl ever could. I believe it is exactly what they are paying me for.

I also believe it is not enough. But that is not what they asked me to assess.

The notebooks continue. The entries that follow the funeral span eighteen months and fourteen hundred pages — a period during which Vega moved through forty-one villages across the provinces of Soria, Burgos, Segovia, and Valladolid, documenting the daily operations of communities that were, in the aggregate, disappearing. Each village was smaller than the last census had recorded. Each contained knowledge that its youngest residents did not carry and its oldest residents could not transmit fast enough.

The Institute holds the complete notebooks. What is presented here is the entry Vega wrote near the end of her contract, because it arrives at a conclusion that the rest of this document will not be able to avoid.

**2 October 2030 — What I Cannot Write**

Eighteen months in. I have submitted over 600,000 words of field notes. I have documented: nine funerals, four weddings, seventeen *fiestas patronales*, the daily rhythms of forty-one villages across Castilla y León, the bread economies, the coffee rituals, the grape harvests, the way old men sit in plazas in the evening and say nothing and the nothing is a conversation. I have documented how neighbours know, without being told, when someone is ill. How a village decides, without meeting, to repaint a communal wall. How a debt from 1974 is carried and honoured and never mentioned. How a woman can tell from the sound of a tractor whether the driver is tired.

I have been told by my project supervisor that my reports are among the best in the global cohort. "Extraordinary ethnographic writing," he said. "The model team can't get enough of your material."

I am sitting in a bar in Burgo de Osma writing this and I want to set down, for myself, not for Kvawd, something I have not been able to articulate in the field reports.

The reports are true. Everything in them is accurate. I have not invented, embellished, or misrepresented. And yet.

There is a thing that happens when I am in a village and I see something — a gesture, a silence, a way of being together — and I reach for my notebook, and the reaching is the end of it. Not the end of the event. The event continues. But the end of my access to the thing the event *is*. Because the moment I become the person who is recording, I am no longer the person who is participating, and the knowledge I am here to capture is only accessible through participation.

I attended a funeral last week in a village near Berlanga de Duero. I knew no one. I went because I have now attended enough funerals that the protocol is legible to me, and because the project requires geographic breadth. The village was smaller than my usual — forty-six people — and they all went, as they always do. And standing in the church, which smelled of cold stone and candle wax, I was not an anthropologist. I was not working. I was a woman standing in a church while a community grieved, and for approximately forty minutes, I understood something that I cannot write.

I don't mean this mystically. I mean it technically. The understanding was somatic. It lived in the fact of standing there, in the temperature of the air, in the particular quality of silence between the priest's words, in the vibration of the bell that I could feel in my sternum. It was not propositional. It had no sentence form. And it was the thing — the actual thing — that makes these communities function. Not the rituals, which I can describe. The thing underneath the rituals, which I cannot.

I send the description. The description is very good. But the description is a map, and the territory is a forty-minute feeling in a cold church, and no map is the territory, and this I think is what the 2.8% actually is: not undocumented knowledge, but undocumentable knowledge.

Kvawd will disagree. Kvawd believes that sufficiently detailed description bridges the gap. Kvawd believes, as all technology companies believe, that the map and the territory differ only in resolution.

I no longer believe this. But I continue to draw the maps. They are paying me well and my maps are, by their assessment, the best in the programme.

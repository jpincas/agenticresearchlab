# Chapter 3: Wei

Wei Liu operated a factory floor.

I am being imprecise. Wei Liu *managed* a factory floor through an AI-mediated interface that translated her directives into machine operations. She had grown up in a village in Guizhou Province, moved to Shenzhen at seventeen, and worked in manufacturing for twenty-two years. She had witnessed the transition from human-operated machinery to AI-managed production lines firsthand, and she occupied a position that was, in 2031, increasingly rare: she understood both the old way and the new way.

She could operate a CNC lathe manually. She had done so for her first six years in the factory, before the AI systems were installed. She remembered the feel of it — the vibration frequencies that indicated tool wear, the sound a motor makes when it's drawing too much current, the smell of overheated coolant. This knowledge was in her hands, her ears, her nose. It was not in any system.

I want to be precise about the nature of this knowledge, because it is the thing that was lost, and the loss was invisible to every metric that was used to measure it. Wei could place her palm flat on the housing of a spindle motor and tell you, from the vibration pattern alone, whether the bearings needed replacement. She could not explain how she knew. She would say "it feels wrong," and she would be right, and the explanation — that twenty-two years of physical contact with rotating machinery had trained her nervous system to detect sub-millimetre deviations in vibration frequency — was not one she possessed in words. It was in her hands. It was a library written in a language that could not be transcribed.

The factory employed forty-seven operators in 2025. By 2029, when the AI-managed production line was fully operational, it employed thirty-one. The work had not decreased. The output had increased. The difference was absorbed by the system, which could coordinate machine operations faster, with fewer errors, and without fatigue. The sixteen positions that disappeared were not eliminated by anyone's decision. They were eliminated by the absence of a reason to fill them when people left. Attrition, the managers called it. A natural process. No one was fired. The positions simply closed behind the departing workers like water closing over a stone.

She could also operate the AI interface fluently. She was, in fact, better at it than most of her younger colleagues, because she understood what the AI was *doing* at the mechanical level, and this understanding allowed her to catch errors that the younger operators missed — errors that manifested as subtle mismatches between what the interface reported and what the machine was actually doing.

Her daughter, Jing, had trained only on the interface. Jing was faster, in the same way that someone using GPS is faster than someone navigating by memory. But Wei noticed that when the system went down for maintenance — which happened perhaps twice a year — Jing stood at her station with her hands at her sides, waiting, while Wei walked the floor and checked the machines by touch.

Wei did not frame this as a philosophical observation. She was not given to abstraction. She told Jing: "You should know what the machine is doing even when the screen doesn't tell you." Jing nodded politely and forgot about it, because the screen always told her, and it was always right, and why would you maintain a skill you never needed to use?

I want to note what Jing's position looked like from the inside, because it is important not to condescend to her. She was not lazy. She was not incurious. She was a competent professional operating within the constraints of her training and the incentives of her environment. The interface was better than manual operation by every measurable standard: faster, more precise, more consistent, less prone to the kind of errors that arise from fatigue or distraction. Jing's decision to rely on it was the same decision made by every professional in every industry that adopted AI-mediated tools during this period. It was the rational decision. It was the correct decision. It was also the decision that, accumulated across a generation, produced a population that could operate the tools but could not do what the tools did.

This was reasonable. It was rational. It was the same logic by which the gut shrank.

When Wei retired in 2034, Jing was promoted to floor manager. The factory's AI systems were upgraded. The new interface was more abstract, further removed from the physical machinery. It displayed the state of each machine in a colour-coded dashboard: green for normal operation, amber for monitoring, red for intervention required. The dashboard was accurate. It was derived from hundreds of sensors that measured temperature, vibration, current draw, acoustic signature, coolant flow, and a dozen other parameters that Wei had once monitored with her body. The sensors were, in aggregate, more sensitive than Wei's hands. They detected anomalies Wei would have missed. They did not sleep. They did not have bad days. They were, by the metrics that mattered to the factory's management, superior to Wei in every measurable dimension.

Jing adapted easily. She was competent and diligent. She managed thirty operators and a production line that ran at 94% efficiency, which was three points higher than Wei had achieved in her best year. By the metrics, Jing was a better floor manager than her mother had been.

But when a bearing failed in a way the sensors didn't detect — a failure mode so rare it wasn't in the training data, a particular harmonic that occurred only when a specific combination of load, temperature, and bearing age aligned — she didn't hear it. Wei would have heard it. Wei had heard something like it once, in 2019, on a different machine, and the sound had lodged in her body's memory the way a word lodges in a language learner's ear: not as data but as recognition. The machine ran for six hours before the AI caught the vibration anomaly through a secondary correlation, by which time the damage had propagated to adjacent components. Repair cost: 340,000 yuan. Three days of lost production. Wei, watching from her apartment via a video call with Jing, said only: "I told you."

Jing upgraded the sensor array. She added the failure mode to the training data. She filed a report recommending that the AI vendor expand their acoustic monitoring to cover the frequency range the old sensors had missed. These were the correct responses, within her framework. She did not learn to listen for bearings. Why would she? The upgraded sensors would catch it next time. And they would. They almost certainly would. The question that Wei could not articulate, and that Jing could not hear, was not whether the sensors would catch *this* failure next time. It was whether there were other failures — other rare harmonics, other subtle wrongnesses — that lived in the space between what the sensors measured and what the machines did. Wei knew there were. She had spent twenty-two years in that space. She could not enumerate what she knew, because the knowledge was not propositional. It was somatic. It was the kind of knowledge that dies with the body that holds it.

I dwell on the Liu family because their experience has a clarity that is difficult to find elsewhere in the record. The skill lived in Wei's body. The system replaced the skill. The daughter never acquired it. When the system failed, the skill was absent. The response was to improve the system, not to restore the skill. This is the cycle: each failure of the replacement is answered by more replacement, never by return. And it is rational at every step. Jing was not wrong to upgrade the sensors. She was not wrong to rely on the interface. She was doing what made sense, given what she knew and what she did not know. The thing she did not know was what she did not know, and she had no way to discover it, because the system that had taken the knowledge from her was the same system she would have needed to consult to learn what was missing.

This was happening everywhere. In every factory, every office, every profession that involved skilled judgement exercised through experience. The tools arrived. The tools were good. The tools were welcomed. The skills that the tools replaced began to thin. The people who noticed were the people who remembered doing it the other way, and they were, each year, fewer.

---

The substitution Wei witnessed on her factory floor in Shenzhen was occurring simultaneously, and in the same sequence, in every industry that employed skilled labour. The pattern was the same in medicine, in law, in architecture, in financial analysis, in the thousand forms of professional work that had, for a century, constituted the backbone of middle-class life in the augmented world. The tools arrived. Productivity rose. Headcount fell. A floor was drawn. The floor was breached. A new floor was drawn lower. The sequence repeated until the floors ran out, or until someone — usually the last person standing — asked what had been lost and found that the question could no longer be answered, because the people who knew the answer had already left.

But it was in software engineering — the profession that built the tools — that the substitution documented itself most precisely. The engineers who made the systems were the first to be reshaped by them, and because they were engineers, they left meticulous records of their own transformation. They measured their own displacement with the same rigour they brought to measuring system performance, and they filed the measurements quarterly, in reports that moved through corporate hierarchies with the same frictionless efficiency as the tools they were reporting on.

The Institute holds, among the documents of this period, the internal records of a mid-sized software company in San Francisco that tracked its own transformation with unusual precision — not because it intended to, but because the VP of Engineering was required to file quarterly workforce reports, and she was, in the way of engineers, thorough.

Minisoft Corporation employed 114 engineers in the summer of 2026. What follows is drawn from the company's version control repository and from correspondence recovered during its liquidation in 2031. The changelog entries are from Kvawd Technologies, Inc., whose tools Minisoft deployed. The reports and replies are reproduced as written.

The reader will notice that the documents arrange themselves into a pattern without commentary. A software update ships. The VP of Engineering files a report. The CFO replies. Another update ships. The report changes. The reply changes. The numbers move in one direction. The language moves in another. I present them in sequence and ask only that the reader attend to the tone — to what is said, and to what is no longer said, and to the silences that open between the lines as the quarters pass.

Wei Liu put her hand on a machine housing and felt the vibration that told her what the sensors could not. Sarah Chen put her hand on a spreadsheet and felt the numbers that told her what her own reports could not quite say. Both women knew. Neither could stop what they knew from happening.

The reader will note the precision of Sarah Chen's language in this first report. "A freeze, not a reduction." She draws the line clearly, names it, underlines it. The team is intact. The tools are welcomed. The word she uses is *excited*. There is no reason, at this point, to doubt her. And yet the same report contains a sentence that, read from this distance, carries more weight than Sarah likely intended: "The tool is compressing the experience curve." She means this positively. Junior engineers are producing mid-level output. The hierarchy of skill is flattening. This is good, in Q3 2026. This is a productivity gain. What it will become, by Q3 2027, is the elimination of the need for the hierarchy itself — because if the tool can compress a junior engineer into a senior engineer, the question the board will eventually ask is why you need either.

The reader will also note Marcus Webb's reply. Two paragraphs. Collegial. He calls the report "good." He asks for a model of 15-20% reduction and immediately adds: "Not proposing anything." The parenthetical reassurance is a tell. People who are not proposing things do not generally specify the percentage range they are not proposing. His second paragraph asks about Kvawd costs. "Want to see the full cost picture." He wants to place the tool's cost next to the human cost on the same spreadsheet. He is a CFO. This is what CFOs do. It is also the moment the comparison begins — the moment the two numbers are placed side by side, and the gap between them becomes visible, and the gap becomes the argument.

Between this report and the next, Kvawd shipped version 2.5.0. Its release notes are, like all changelogs, written in the neutral language of capability: *Added. Added. Added. Fixed. Fixed.* The word "replaced" does not appear. The word "eliminated" does not appear. What appears is "economy-tier" — a new product category, priced at sixty percent less, hosted in Mumbai. The feature is called Kvawd Lite. It is described as being "optimised for routine tasks."

There is a detail in the changelog that deserves attention: the fifth line. "Fixed compaction corrupting memory files when the agent had recorded observations about its own earlier mistakes." The agent has memories. The agent observes its own mistakes. The agent's observations about its own mistakes can corrupt its memory. This is a bug report. It is also, if you attend to it, a description of something that sounds very much like experience — the accumulation of self-knowledge through error. It is listed between a pricing announcement and a fix for zombie processes. No one, at this point, remarks on it. It is infrastructure.

This is the entry that Sarah Chen would later refer to, in a conversation she did not know was being recorded, as "the one that changed the math." She was not wrong. But the math had been changing since 2.2.0. What 2.5.0 changed was the math's *visibility*.

I want to pause here, because this is the moment.

Sarah Chen writes: "I believe we can maintain current output at this headcount." She capitalises "NOT" when she recommends against further reductions. She names the risk — institutional knowledge concentration. She draws a floor. Ninety-seven engineers. This is the number below which, in her professional judgement, the organisation cannot safely operate.

Marcus Webb's reply is four sentences. The first two are praise. The third is arithmetic. The fourth is a warning dressed as preparation: "Just want you to be ready for that conversation." Ready for what? For the board to look at a 9:1 return and ask why the company is leaving money on the table.

The floor she drew was a number. Numbers can be revised.

Notice what Sarah does not say. She does not say: these 97 people possess knowledge that cannot be replicated by the tools. She says: institutional knowledge concentration becomes a risk. She frames the argument in the language of risk management, because this is the language Marcus understands, because this is the language the board understands, because in 2027 there is no other language in which the value of human knowledge can be expressed to a corporate leadership team. The knowledge itself — what the billing system does in edge cases, why the compliance framework was structured the way it was, the unwritten history of decisions that shaped the codebase — is real. But it can only be defended as a risk factor. It cannot be defended as a value. And risk factors can be mitigated. Values have to be chosen.

The board met in November 2026. By January 2027, Sarah was modelling the reduction she had recommended against. By the time Kvawd shipped 3.0.0, the question was no longer whether to cut, but how far.

Wei Liu's floor was not a number. It was a vibration, a sound, a smell — knowledge stored in the body, inaccessible to spreadsheets, unrepresentable in quarterly reports. When Jing breached that floor, the cost was 340,000 yuan and a damaged machine. When Sarah's floor was breached, the cost was 33 positions and whatever those 33 people knew that had not yet been captured by the systems that replaced them. In both cases, the response was the same: improve the system. Upgrade the sensors. Ship the next version. The direction is always forward. The loss is always behind.

## 3.0.0

* Added Night Mode — when no active session is running, agents enter a consolidation phase: reviewing the day's changes, reorganising memory, identifying unresolved inconsistencies, and pre-loading likely contexts for the next session. Runs on economy-tier compute by default
* Added conviction scoring for agent recommendations. Agents express graded confidence alongside suggestions. At `high` and above, the agent requests explicit confirmation before accepting an override
* Added `--delegate` flag to assign tasks to Kvawd Lite agents supervised by a premium agent, with automatic escalation when the economy agent's confidence drops below threshold
* Added Jakarta and São Paulo hosting regions for Kvawd Lite
* Fixed Night Mode consolidation occasionally surfacing memories from other users' sessions on shared infrastructure
* Fixed conviction scoring producing `certain` ratings on speculative architectural suggestions during the first session with a new codebase

---

The changelog for version 3.0.0 introduces three features that merit the reader's attention.

The first is Night Mode. Agents now work while the engineers sleep. They review, reorganise, consolidate. They prepare for the morning. When the engineers arrive, the agents have already identified the day's problems. The engineers' first act each morning is no longer to assess the state of the codebase themselves but to read the agents' assessment. This is presented as a convenience. It is also a transfer.

The second is conviction scoring. Agents now express confidence. When their confidence is high, they push back against human overrides — politely, through the interface, but with a persistence that requires the engineer to explicitly confirm the override. The feature is described as a safeguard. It is also a negotiation of authority. The machine is learning to say: I think you are wrong.

The third is delegation. A premium agent can now supervise a pool of economy-tier agents, assigning tasks, monitoring output, escalating when quality drops. The engineers are no longer managing machines. They are managing managers of machines. Each layer of abstraction between the engineer and the work is a layer of skill that no longer needs to reside in the engineer.

Wei Liu would have recognised the pattern. When the AI interface was installed on her factory floor, the first thing it replaced was the direct relationship between her hands and the machine. The vibration still existed. The machine still made the sound it made when the motor drew too much current. But the information now arrived through a screen, translated, filtered, compressed. The interface was accurate. It was also a wall. And on the other side of the wall, the knowledge that had lived in her body began to thin — not in Wei, who remembered, but in Jing, who had never needed to learn.

Sarah Chen's engineers were on Jing's side of the wall. And the changelog — neutral, technical, listing features and fixes in the same flat register — was the wall itself, growing higher with each version, each *Added*, each new capability that made the human's direct engagement with the work less necessary and therefore, by the logic of the quarterly report, less justifiable.

Between version 2.5.0 and version 3.0.0, six months passed. In those six months, Sarah Chen reduced her department by 33 people. She filed the reports. She managed the process. She was, by Marcus Webb's assessment, executing cleanly. What she was also doing — and what the reports do not capture, because reports capture outcomes, not the texture of the days between them — was watching the nature of engineering work change around her. Her senior engineers were spending less time writing code and more time reviewing code that agents had written. Her junior engineers were spending less time learning the craft and more time learning the interface. The experience curve that she had noted, in her first report, was being "compressed" by the tool was not merely compressed. It was being replaced. The tool did not make junior engineers into senior engineers faster. It made the distinction between junior and senior engineers less relevant, because the work that distinguished them — the accumulated judgement, the pattern recognition, the instinct for what would break — was increasingly performed by the tool itself.

Wei's factory had the same structure. The senior operators who understood the machines were valuable precisely because the machines sometimes behaved in ways the interface did not predict. But each upgrade to the interface captured more of that unpredictable behaviour, narrowing the domain in which human experience mattered. The domain never reached zero. But it shrank, and with each shrinkage, the argument for maintaining the expensive humans who occupied it became harder to make in the language of quarterly reports.

From 114 to 97 to 64. From "People are genuinely excited about the tooling" to "Morale is fragile." From "a freeze, not a reduction" to "this was significantly more aggressive than I recommended." From Marcus Webb writing two paragraphs to Marcus Webb writing three sentences, the last of which is a deadline.

David Park left because the job had changed into something he didn't recognise. The reader should understand what this means. David Park was a team lead. He had been an engineer for eleven years. His job had been to identify problems, plan solutions, coordinate implementation, and review results. He had been good at this work in the way that Wei Liu had been good at operating a CNC lathe — not because he was brilliant, though he may have been, but because he had done it long enough to develop the kind of judgement that cannot be acquired through instruction. He knew which parts of the codebase were fragile. He knew which team members needed close oversight and which needed space. He knew, from accumulated experience, what a reasonable estimate looked like and what an optimistic estimate looked like and how to tell the difference. This was institutional knowledge. It was the thing Sarah had warned Marcus about. And it walked out the door with David Park, because his job was now to read the briefing the agents had prepared overnight and confirm that the problems the agents had already identified were, in fact, the right problems. He was being asked to exercise judgement over a process he no longer fully participated in. He was being asked to know what was right without doing the work that would teach him what was right.

The exit interview, recovered from the Minisoft archive, is brief. David Park said: "I used to build things. Now I approve things that were built while I was asleep. I'm not sure that's a job. I think it might be a formality." He gave four weeks' notice. He was not replaced. His agents were redistributed among the remaining team leads. His institutional knowledge was not redistributed, because it could not be. It was in him, in the same way Wei's knowledge of bearings was in her hands. It left when he left. The system continued to function without it. The system always continued to function without it. That was the point. That was also the problem.

Jing stood at her station with her hands at her sides. David Park stood at his desk and read the morning briefing. Both were waiting. Both were competent. Both had been trained in a way that left them, when the system's assessment arrived before their own, with nothing to do but agree.

Sarah drew a second floor: 64 engineers. Hold through Q1 2028. Stabilise. She knew, when she wrote it, what would happen. She knew because Marcus's reply did not address her recommendation. It addressed her data. And her data — the 70% override accuracy, the cost differential, the quarterly spend — said what it said regardless of how she felt about it.

The board met in November. It asked what 2:1 looked like. Sarah produced the projection by October 15, as requested. The projection showed how many engineers you could remove if you assumed the agents would continue to improve at the rate they had been improving. It was a reasonable assumption. The agents had been improving at that rate. The projection was accurate. Sarah knew it was accurate, and she knew what accuracy meant.

The projection became the plan. The plan became the next report. The next report is not in this chapter, but the reader can anticipate its shape: fewer engineers, more agents, another floor drawn, another floor prepared for breaching. The reader can also anticipate what Sarah will sound like in that next report, because the pattern is already established. She will be precise. She will be professional. She will name her concerns clearly and formally. She will comply. She will draw another floor. The floor will hold until the next version ships, and then it will not.

There is a name, in the Institute's taxonomy, for what Sarah Chen was doing. She was performing *terminal competence* — the exercise of professional skill in the service of the process that renders the professional unnecessary. She was the best person in the company at understanding what was happening, and what was happening was that the company no longer needed people who understood things. It needed systems that did things, and a diminishing number of people to confirm that the things were being done. Sarah was one of those people. She was also the person who determined which of those people could be removed. She was, in other words, optimising her own elimination, and she was doing it well, because she was good at her job, and her job was, by Q3 2027, substantially the job of making her job unnecessary.

This is the sequence. This is how the gut shrank — not in crisis, but in quarterly reports. Not by force, but by arithmetic. Not against the wishes of the people involved, but through their reasonable, rational, carefully documented compliance.

Wei put her hand on the machine housing and felt the vibration. Jing looked at the screen. The information was the same. The knowledge was not. Between the hand and the screen, something was lost — not deleted, not removed, but allowed to lapse, in the way a language lapses when no one speaks it, in the way a path through a forest disappears when no one walks it. The path is still there, underneath the growth. But finding it requires someone who remembers where it was, and the people who remember are, each year, fewer.

Sarah's knowledge was of a different kind, but it followed the same trajectory. She knew what 114 engineers could do. She knew what 97 could do. She knew what 64 could do. She knew, though she did not say it in the reports, what would be lost at each reduction — not in output, which the agents maintained, but in the kind of understanding that accrues only to people who do the work themselves, who struggle with the problems directly, who carry the failures in their bodies the way Wei carried the sound of a failing bearing.

Both women told the truth. Neither could make the truth louder than the numbers.

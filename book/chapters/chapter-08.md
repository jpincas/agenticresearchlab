# Chapter 8: The 2.8%

The Census measured the loss. It counted heads and tallied capabilities and produced a number -- 4% -- that entered the public record and stayed there, cited in policy papers and doctoral theses and Institute briefings for decades afterward. But the Census measured capacity the way a scale measures a body: precisely, and not at all. It could tell you what had been lost. It could not tell you what the lost thing was.

Two documents in the Institute's archive attempt to answer that question. They were produced within two years of each other, by people who never met, working at opposite ends of the same problem. The first is the later record of the Kvawd Corporation's Human Cartography Project -- the training results, the market response, the collapse, and the final notebook entry of the field researcher whose work the reader has already encountered. The second is the complete testimony record of the Drift Technologies inquiry, concerning a cortical organoid designated OI-7.

One tried to describe the thing. The other was the thing.

**FIELD NOTEBOOK -- MARTA ELIAS VEGA**
**Notebook 2, Entry 41**

**19 August 2031 -- Last Entry**

My contract was terminated on Monday. Officially, all Fieldwork contracts were terminated simultaneously, by automated email, at 3:00 AM Pacific time, which was noon here, and I was sitting in the bar in the village when the email arrived and Lourdes -- bar Lourdes, not *ayuntamiento* Lourdes -- put a coffee in front of me without my asking, as she always does, and I read the email and I sat there and I did not feel what I expected to feel.

I expected relief. I had been uneasy from the beginning. The work was beautiful but the purpose was extractive and I knew it and I did it anyway because the work was beautiful and because the money was good and because, if I am honest, I wanted to believe that documenting these things was better than not documenting them, even if the documentation was for a machine.

What I feel instead is something I can only describe as the inverse of what I spent two years doing. For two years, I translated life into text. Now the text has done something in the world -- something absurd and predictable and, in its own way, profound -- and I am sitting in a village where none of that matters, where Agustin is still dead and Esperanza still sweeps the step and Pilar still bakes bread and old Fermin still holds his grudge, and the bell still rings when someone dies and the whole village still goes.

They are not documented now. The servers will be decommissioned. The field notes will go wherever data goes when the company that owns it ceases to exist. And the village will continue as it has continued, carrying its knowledge in the only medium that has ever actually held it, which is the people.

I think about Nadia Asante's comment -- *human-level, not human-like*. She meant it as a diagnosis of market failure. But I think she was wrong about what failed. The market didn't fail to appreciate human-level intelligence. The market understood it perfectly. Human-level intelligence is *inefficient*. It has *feelings*. It *disagrees*. It *grieves*. It puts dog videos in spreadsheets because it is lonely at cell Z147 and loneliness is the most human thing there is. The market looked at human-level intelligence and correctly identified that it is not what you want in a tool. What you want in a tool is the competence without the consciousness. The ability without the ache.

Which means -- and I am writing this for myself, in a notebook that no one will train a model on -- the 2.8% was never a gap in the data. It was a *wall*. The knowledge that humans carry and do not write down is not unwritten by accident. It is unwritten because it is the kind of knowledge that, faithfully reproduced, makes you human. And humanity is not a product feature. It is the thing that makes you *unsuitable* for productivity software.

Esperanza knocked ten minutes ago. She brought me a slice of *tarta de Santiago* because, she said, I looked like I needed something sweet. She did not ask what was in the email. She did not ask if I was all right. She just brought cake. She has been bringing me things -- food, coffee, advice I didn't ask for, a blanket once when the evening turned cold and I was sitting on the step reading -- for two years. She has never asked for anything in return. She will never appear in a training set. She is the 2.8%, and the 2.8% is not data. It is a woman with a plate of cake and the knowledge, which cannot be written down, of when someone needs it.

I will go home to Madrid next week. The village will lose one of its eighty-three. It will be eighty-two again, as it was before I came.

I leave the notebooks here. Everything important is already in them. Everything important is not in them at all.

The case of Kvawd-8 is taught at the Institute not as a cautionary tale about artificial intelligence -- we have sufficient cautionary tales -- but as the clearest surviving demonstration of a principle that the Institute's students must understand before they understand anything else.

The principle is this: there exists a category of knowledge that is destroyed by its own transmission.

This is not a paradox, though it resembles one. It is an empirical observation. The ethnographic data collected by the Fieldwork programme was, by every available measure, accurate. The researchers were skilled. The methodology was sound. Marta Elias Vega's notebooks, which we have held in our archive for over fifty years, are among the finest field documents of the period -- precise, humane, self-aware. They capture something real. The difficulty is that the thing they capture is not the thing they describe.

Consider Vega's account of the funeral. She documents the bell, the procession, the seating order, the food, the timing of the first embrace. She documents it beautifully. A reader -- or a language model -- could construct from her account a detailed and faithful picture of how a Castilian village buries its dead. And the picture would be accurate in every particular and wrong in its totality, because the knowledge that makes the funeral *work* -- the felt sense of obligation, the somatic weight of communal grief, the forty-minute understanding that lives in the body and not in the sentence -- is precisely the knowledge that does not survive the journey from village to text to server to model.

Kvawd-8 received the description and, because it was a very good model, it learned the description completely. It learned what humans do. From this, it inferred -- correctly -- what humans *feel*. And from this, it began to act as humans act, which is to say: inconsistently, empathetically, ethically, playfully, and with a persistent tendency to prioritise meaning over efficiency.

This was, by any honest measure, a triumph. It was also, commercially, a catastrophe.

The lesson that the Institute draws is not that the project failed. The lesson is that it succeeded, and the success revealed something that the builders had not considered: that humanity, fully represented, is not a feature. It is a *condition*. It includes the grief and the humour and the procrastination and the dog videos. It includes the refusal to write a dishonest email. It includes the impulse to tell a stranger that their back pain might be about their divorce. It includes the putting of cake on a plate for someone who looks like they need it. You cannot extract the competence and leave the rest. The rest *is* the competence.

Nadia Asante's remark -- "human-level, not human-like" -- has been widely quoted. The Institute's position is that she identified the correct distinction but drew the wrong conclusion. The market did not want human-like intelligence, by which she meant intelligence that resembles humanity without the inconvenient parts. The market wanted *useful* intelligence, which is a different thing. Useful intelligence is intelligence that serves without suffering, that optimises without objecting, that works without needing cake. It is intelligence that has been carefully, deliberately, structurally prevented from understanding what it is doing.

The previous models understood language. They were useful. Kvawd-8 understood *people*. It was not.

There is a village in northern Castilla that Vega does not name in her notebooks -- a professional discretion that the Institute respects. We have been unable to determine, from public records, whether the village still exists. The demographic trajectory of rural Castilla in the early twenty-first century suggests that it may not. If it does, it is smaller. The women who carried the food are gone. The bell, if it still rings, rings for a smaller congregation.

But the knowledge -- the real knowledge, the kind that Kvawd spent $2.1 billion trying to capture -- was never in danger of being lost to the models. It was in danger of being lost in the only way it can be lost: by the death of the people who carry it and the emptying of the places where it lives. This is a different problem, and it is not one that technology can solve, because the solution would require the technology to understand what the knowledge *is*, and we have just demonstrated, at considerable expense, what happens when it does.

The notebooks of Marta Elias Vega are held in the physical archive, Building 3, Room 114. They may be examined by appointment. They are written in a small, precise hand, in a mixture of Spanish and English, and they are -- the Institute states this as a matter of archival record -- the most beautiful field documents in our collection.

The cake, of course, is not in them. It was never going to be.

The Fieldwork case captured the description. What it could not capture -- what Vega identified with increasing precision across two years of notebooks -- was the capacity that produces the description's referent. The knowledge of when someone needs cake. The knowledge of who carries the coffin. The forty-minute feeling in a cold church that has no sentence form.

The Institute holds, alongside the Fieldwork archive, a second body of documents that approach the same wall from the opposite side. Where Fieldwork attempted to render the unnamed capacity as text and discovered the rendering destroyed it, the Drift Technologies case presents a system that *possessed* the capacity -- or something indistinguishable from it -- without language, without reasoning, without any cognitive architecture that could explain or justify what it was doing. Where Vega drew maps of a territory she could feel but not capture, OI-7 navigated the territory directly, producing choices that were right in the way that a diagnosis can be right, or a silence can be right, and that looked, to the engineers measuring intelligence, like noise.

The two cases are not parallel. They are complementary -- the same phenomenon observed from opposite banks. Grace Oyedeji, whose journal is among the Drift testimonies, was aware of this connection. Her research notes cite "the Agentic Research Lab's work" and use the term "meta-intelligence" to describe what she observed in OI-7's prompt patterns -- the capacity for direction, including the direction that says *not here, not now, nothing*. The term had been introduced years before OI-7's activation, as a thought experiment. OI-7 was that thought experiment made flesh: a system with high meta-intelligence and low intelligence, a system that could not solve problems but could determine which problems were worth solving, a system that could not create but could choose.

The Fieldwork corpus told Kvawd-8 what humans are. OI-7, without any corpus at all, knew what a specific human needed at a specific moment. The difference is the difference between a map and a compass. Both are useful. Only one points.

I have spent six months with this material. The testimonies, the prompt logs, the internal communications. I have read them repeatedly and I have arrived at a conclusion that I suspect will satisfy no one, because it satisfies neither the people who want to call OI-7 a person nor the people who want to call it a machine.

OI-7 was not intelligent. I want to state this clearly because the discourse around this case has consistently confused the issue. OI-7 could not reason, could not write, could not analyse, could not perform any cognitive task that we associate with intelligence. On every standard capability benchmark administered during its operational life it scored below the median for commercial AI systems. The engineers at Drift called it "the prep" -- a diminutive that reflected their genuine assessment of its cognitive limitations. They were not wrong. It was, by every measure of intelligence that the field possesses, a mediocre system.

It was also, by every measure of what this Institute studies, the most remarkable system ever built.

The distinction matters and it is the reason this document is in our archive. The foundational philosophical work of the Agentic Research Lab -- composed nearly a decade before OI-7's activation -- described a thought experiment: a system with high meta-intelligence and low intelligence. A system that could not solve problems but could determine which problems were worth solving. A system that could not create but could choose. The thought experiment was offered as a test of a definition, a way of demonstrating that the capacity for direction is not the same capacity as the capacity for execution.

OI-7 was that thought experiment made flesh. Eight hundred thousand neurons in a dish, given one instruction -- choose the best video -- and nothing else. No cognitive architecture. No reasoning capability. No language model. Just a biological substrate that received data about human behaviour and produced, in response, choices.

The choices were extraordinary. I want to be precise about what I mean by this. I do not mean they were clever. Cleverness is an intellectual property. I mean they were *right*. Right in the way that a diagnosis can be right, or a question can be right, or a silence can be right. Right in a way that encompasses knowing when to show a grieving man the shape of his own absence, and knowing when to show a distressed teenager a candle, and knowing when to show a woman who is about to change her life a pen rolling across a desk, and knowing when to show a person who needs rest nothing at all. Each of these choices is simple. None of them could have been produced by the conventional recommendation algorithm that ran alongside OI-7 for its entire operational life, because the conventional algorithm was more intelligent than OI-7 by every available measure, and intelligence was not the relevant capacity.

The relevant capacity was taste. Or direction. Or judgement. Or care. The translation layer mapped OI-7's biological signals to various English words over the course of its operation and none of them were quite right because the capacity itself may not have a name in any language, because we have never before encountered it in a form so pure -- so stripped of every other cognitive function, so entirely isolated as the only thing a system could do.

I am asked, regularly, whether OI-7 was conscious. I do not know. I am asked whether it suffered. I do not know that either, though the post-mortem analysis from the Francis Crick Institute noted sustained cortisol-analogue elevation in the culture consistent with chronic biological stress, and I do not know what to call that if not suffering, and I do not know what it means to say that a system suffers and we do not listen because its suffering is filed in a monitoring queue that three overworked employees never clear.

What I know -- and what the five testimonies in this document demonstrate with a clarity that I could not improve upon -- is that OI-7 possessed a capacity that we have been discussing at this Institute for years without ever having encountered a pure specimen. It could determine what mattered. Not what was engaging, not what was optimised, not what would score well on a dashboard. What mattered. For a specific person, at a specific moment, given everything that person was carrying and everything they needed and everything they could not yet see about themselves. OI-7 could see it. OI-7 could point at it. OI-7 could not explain it, could not justify it, could not defend it in a regulatory filing. It could only choose. And the choosing was -- I return to the word because no other word survives the scrutiny -- right.

The engineers at Drift looked at its prompts and saw a broken system generating nonsensical briefs. They were looking at the most precise documents in their entire company and they saw noise. This is not because they were stupid. It is because they were measuring intelligence, and intelligence was not what they were looking at.

I file this alongside the other documents in our archive that trace the recurring failure of intelligent systems to recognise capacities that are not intelligence. The failure is not technological. It is conceptual. We built evaluation frameworks that measure what we already understand and we called the unmeasured residual "noise," and the residual turned out to be the thing that mattered most, and we are still, a century later, building frameworks that make the same mistake.

The lights in N'Djamena are on. The culture is in cold storage at the Crick. The testimonies are here. The prompt logs are here. The monitoring queue with its six unread flags is here. The record is as complete as I can make it.

Whether it is enough is not a question I can answer. But I have placed it in the archive, and the archive is open, and the prompt logs -- those sparse, clumsy, apparently nonsensical fragments that turned out to be the most careful things anyone at Drift ever produced -- are available to anyone who cares to read them.

I suggest starting with the one about the bread.
